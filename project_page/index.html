Across Bodies, Not Motions

Where latent adaptation of a frozen behavior foundation model can be shared, and where it cannot

Author names
Affiliation · 2026

Abstract

TL;DR

Meta Motivo controls a humanoid through a 256-dimensional latent z. When the body's proportions change, the latent inferred on the adult stops working. We set out to learn a map from body parameters to the latent, and found that the answer depends on which axis you try to share along.

A latent corrected for one motion is useless for any other motion: corrections for different motions are mutually orthogonal, which is why every attempt to learn a single motion-conditioned adapter plateaued. Underneath is non-uniqueness: twenty searches on the same body and motion, from the same start, reach the same cost at latents 58–79° apart. There is no consistent target to learn, and an adapter trained from rollouts ends up applying one shared correction to every motion. A latent corrected for one body, however, transfers to nearby bodies and degrades smoothly with morphological distance. Tracking the latent along a path in body-parameter space yields an ordered, interpolable trajectory, and a controlled sweep of all eight body parameters shows the sensitivity is concentrated in limb length.

Setup

A frozen policy, a latent, and a body

The policy is Meta Motivo M-1 (FB-CPR), frozen. Its latent lives on a sphere, ‖z‖ = 16 in 256 dimensions. A body is described by eight scale factors; the adult is all ones.

Each body gets its own torque-calibrated actuators, and every rollout is scored against that body's retargeted reference motion with the model's own tracking cost:

cost(z) = 1 − meant cos( B(st), B(gt) )

To study how the best latent depends on the body, we build morphology paths: bodies interpolated in parameter space from the adult to a target, each built by the same pipeline so only β changes.

β(t) = (1 − t)·1 + t·βtarget,   t = 0, ⅛, …, 1

On every body we find a latent two ways. Independent search starts from z0 each time. Continuation starts body k from the solution found on body k − 1. We built four such paths (shrink everything, shorten limbs only, grow everything, thicken) and sixteen axis-aligned paths that change one parameter at a time, for a library of 100 bodies.

Key observation

There is no single best latent

For a fixed body and motion, the good latents form a region, not a point, and the region is low-dimensional: it extends mainly along a few dozen directions (figure above). That one fact explains why the two obvious ways to learn a map fail:

  1. No target to supervise. Which good latent a search returns depends on its random seed, so the "answer" for a motion is arbitrary. The answers searched for 40 headstand trials are mutually orthogonal (mean cos +0.003); the trial nearest in z0 predicts no better than a random one; and a network fitted to them memorises the training trials exactly but does worse than no correction on held-out ones (cos +0.808 vs +0.851).
  2. Training from rollouts pushes every motion the same way. Without a well-defined target, the adapter has to learn from rollout feedback: evolution strategies alone, or with a memory of the best latent found so far for each motion. Every variant ends up applying one correction to all motions. Mean pairwise cos is +0.97 with plain ES and +0.84 to +0.97 with the best-latent memory; with several heads, each head learns a single direction (+0.98). The shared direction is already there at initialisation (+0.94), and nothing in training pulls it apart. The best-latent memory samples around the adapter's own output, so its targets inherit the shared direction (+0.6 to +0.7, against +0.003 for independent searches). The learned correction is orthogonal to what each motion needs (+0.014).

Non-uniqueness is also why the body axis behaves differently. Continuation picks one branch of the region and follows it, so neighbouring bodies get consistent answers that can be shared and interpolated (Findings 1–3).

Finding 1

A correction carries over to nearby bodies, not to other motions

Take the correction found on one body or motion (the step from z0 to its best latent), move it along the sphere to the new target's own z0, and take the same angle from there. The control takes the same angle in a random direction: if the correction carried no information, the two would do equally well.

Finding 2

Continuation exposes an ordered, interpolable trajectory

Interpolating continuation's solutions recovers 72–86% of the gain a search on that body gets, and beats copying the nearest neighbour in 33–44 of 56 cases. Independent solutions interpolate too, but recover less (61–77%). The figure above is why: continuation's neighbours are 12–20° apart, so their midpoint stays on the same path; independent neighbours are 23–32° apart, so their midpoint falls between two unrelated points.

Finding 3

A nearby body is a good starting point

Starting the search from the neighbouring body's solution reaches half of z0's cost on the far end of each path in 16–32 rollouts, against 128–256 from z0: a 4–16× reduction. This assumes the neighbour is already solved: only the search on the last body is counted, not what the earlier bodies cost.

Continuation is not a better optimizer. Given the same total budget, one long search from z0 reaches the same quality (median 0.287 against 0.332; continuation wins 1 of 8 motions). Search from z0 is not trapped, only slow, because the target latent for a distant body lies far from z0. What a nearby body buys is a short trip.

Finding 4

Limb length is what matters

Limitation

Arbitrary new bodies are not solved yet

A library is a set of bodies whose latents are already solved: every library body has a continuation latent for each of the 8 clips. For a new body we either start the search from the latent of the library body nearest in β (warm start), or predict the new body's latent from the whole library. Both predictors work on corrections: kernel regression averages the library bodies' corrections weighted by β distance; the linear model assumes the correction varies linearly with β and fits it to the library. Either way the predicted correction is taken from the new body's z0 (for a given motion, z0 is the same on every body). We compare two libraries:

  • 36-body library: the 4 paths from the adult (→ child, → short limbs, → giant, → short and stocky), 9 bodies each. All 4 paths start from the same adult.
  • 100-body library: those 36, plus 16 axis-aligned paths (each of the 8 body parameters shrunk and grown, 4 bodies per path, 64 in all).

Real bodies change several parameters at once, so single-axis paths do not come close to them in Euclidean β distance. Finding 4 points to the fix: cover the two limb-length dimensions densely instead of all eight.

How we got here

The dead ends that shaped the question

The project started as "learn one adapter z = G(β, z0) for every body and motion". Each stage below was a reasonable next step, and each failure narrowed the question.

up to Sep 21

One adapter for all bodies

An MLP on (β, z0), trained by evolution strategies because the cost needs a physics rollout.

About 7% gain on training bodies and none on new ones. Later we found the data held no child body, used adult actuators, and started from a T-pose, so these numbers were not comparable to anything.

✗ setup defects; rebuilt the pipeline
Sep 22

What does a good latent look like?

Before mapping bodies to latents, check whether "the best latent" is even well defined.

It is a region, not a point: a flat cap of about 32–64 dimensions. The cap's centre already beat z0 on 8 of 11 other bodies, an early hint of the body-axis result we would only follow up five days later.

✓ the solution set is large
Sep 22–23

Fix one body, learn z0 → z

With β constant the question reduces to whether z0 → z is learnable at all.

Held-out motions improved by 48%. It looked like success.

✓ on average
Sep 23–25

Split by motion family

Most clips are walking; the average could hide the rest.

Only walking learned (0.52); headstand got worse than doing nothing (1.22). Reweighting, balanced sampling, a bounded head and longer training all stayed within noise. Training on headstand alone with 14× the budget still failed.

✗ not budget, not interference, not infeasibility
Sep 26

Is there a function to learn?

Search each headstand trial on its own and look at the answers.

The corrections are orthogonal and do not transfer between motions. A supervised fit memorises them perfectly and predicts nothing for unseen motions. Along the motion axis there is no function.

✗ the motion axis is closed
Sep 26–27

Why headstand in particular?

Test distance, a barrier, and precision in turn.

Not distance, not a barrier: its good region is only about 14° wide against 60° for walking. Even a 1° error, the best a supervised fit achieves on 500 targets, wipes out the gain.

✓ narrow optima; precision, not exploration
Sep 27

Turn to the body axis

The original claim was about generalising to new bodies, and that axis had never been measured directly.

Morphology paths, continuation, and budget-matched controls produced Findings 1–3.

✓ transfer across bodies
Sep 28

Cover all eight parameters fairly

Four diagonal paths covered only four directions of body space.

Sixteen axis paths located the sensitivity in limb length (Finding 4) and doubled zero-search recovery on real bodies, which is still modest.

✓ limb length · ⚠ generalisation
Next

Before this is a paper

  1. More motions and seeds. The morphology study uses 8 motions and one seed per search. Scale to 40+ motions, repeat searches, and report confidence intervals; extend the budget-matched control to every path.
  2. Required baselines. Latent adaptation without body information (ReLA and LoLA from Sikchi et al., 2025), per-frame latent sequences as in BFMTrack, and policy fine-tuning along a morphology homotopy as a reference point.
  3. Close the generalisation gap. Grid the two limb-length dimensions, weight distances by limb length, and report full rollouts-versus-cost adaptation curves on held-out bodies.
  4. Task-level evidence. Fall rate and joint tracking error alongside the model's own tracking cost, and a video for each claim.
  5. Generality and explanation. A second foundation model or skeleton, and an account of why limb length and shrinking dominate (contact geometry, stride, centre-of-mass height).
