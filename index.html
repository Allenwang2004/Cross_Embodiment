Summary

What this is

Meta Motivo is a behavior foundation model trained only on an adult body. A 256-dimensional latent z selects what it does. We freeze the whole policy and change only z, and ask whether it can drive a child's body (lengths × 0.611, 38 kg against the adult's 72 kg).

We first align the interface between body and policy. The actuators get PD gains and torque limits scaled to the body. The observation is converted by exact reverse retargeting into the equivalent adult observation. The policy's actions are joint target angles, which do not depend on body size, so they need no conversion. To the policy, it is still looking at an adult, one with weaker muscles.

With the interface aligned, the adult's own z0 is already close to usable on the child. Latent search closes the rest, and it is cheap: part of the past motions' corrections lies along a few shared directions. Searching only the top 8 principal directions recovers 83% of a 64-generation full-space search within 8 generations (128 rollouts); with the same budget, all 256 dimensions reach 72% and a random 8-dimensional subspace 70%. These directions come from the child, and on other bodies they help only in the first few generations. Corrections for different motions are nearly orthogonal, and a learned, general adapter does almost nothing on unseen motions, so we search each motion quickly instead.

From the start

What each step changes, and by how much

Step 0 · baseline

Change nothing

Only the body is shrunk to child proportions. The actuators keep the adult settings (PD gains kp 180, kd 2, torque limit ±360 N·m), and the policy reads the child's own observation. Median L_align 1.82, mean joint-angle error 15.6°, and the body ends 2.0 m away from where the reference does.

Step 1 · actuators

Scale the actuators to the body

Adult torques are too strong for a 38 kg body. We scale the PD gains and torque limits to the body: kp 180 → 35.4, kd 2 → 0.31, ±360 → ±70.7 N·m. L_align drops to 1.53 and the drift from 2.0 m to 0.96 m, but the joint-angle error does not improve (17.5°), because the observation the policy sees is still wrong.

Step 2 · observation

Convert the child's state into an adult observation

The policy has only seen adult observations. The child's pelvis height, body positions and velocities are about 60% of the adult's, so the policy sees an adult state that makes no sense. A fixed per-feature multiplier (2–15% error) brings L_align to 1.08. Exact reverse retargeting copies the joint angles, divides root position and linear velocity by 0.611, keeps the ground clearance, and computes the adult observation with humenv's own formula. L_align drops to 0.62 and the joint-angle error halves (8.2°).

Step 3 · latent

Search 5 generations in an 8-dimensional subspace

What remains is the real dynamics difference: mass distribution, torque limits, inertia. That part goes to z. Starting from z0, 5 generations (80 rollouts) bring the median L_align to 0.43.

To the policy, it is controlling an adult with weaker muscles

With the observation in adult units, the policy sees an adult whose posture and velocities make sense. Its actions are PD target joint angles, and a given angle means the same thing on an adult or a child, so actions need no conversion. The only real difference left is dynamics: for the same target angle, the child's joints get there with different speed and force. No conversion can remove that, so z makes up for it.

Fast search

A few generations are enough

The search is an antithetic evolution strategy (ES) with Adam. Each generation draws 8 random directions and tries each with both signs (16 rollouts), estimates a downhill direction from the ranking of the results, and updates z. The cost is cosine + heading + pelvis position (see the box below), plus a small penalty 0.3 (‖z − z0‖/16)² so that z does not wander off for nothing.

z = 16 · normalize( z0 + U c ),   c ∈ ℝ⁸

U holds 8 correction directions: the top 8 principal components of the corrections z* − z0 found by 64-generation searches on 455 other motions. None of the 60 test motions is used to compute U, and 25 of them come from 5 motion types that do not appear in the computation at all. The search starts directly from z0. A step in the subspace is as long as a step in the full space, so speed depends only on the directions.

Why not cosine similarity as the cost

Another common cost uses Meta Motivo's own B space: 1 − mean cos(B(s), B(g)), comparing the embeddings of the rollout and reference states frame by frame. But B reads humenv's 358-dim observation, which is expressed in the pelvis's heading frame and leaves out the pelvis's x and y. Moving a pose 5.8 m and turning it 90° changes the observation by 4e-15. So cosine sees the local posture, the pelvis height and the body-frame velocities, but not which way the body faces or where it is.

As a result, walking in the right direction and slowly turning off course or drifting several metres cost about the same. 20 walking solutions with similar cosine cost ended facing −112° to +22° and 0.2 to 13.4 m from the reference. So with cosine alone, two global error terms have to be added, heading 1 − cos(Δheading) and pelvis position, to keep these in check.

Across bodies

The child's 8 directions on other bodies

U is computed from the child's searches. We used the same U unchanged on four other bodies: teen (smaller than the adult), short_limbed (child-sized, different limb proportions), elderly (close to the adult) and giant (larger than the adult). 24 motions per body, the same cost, and observations converted with each body's own scale (0.835, 0.654, 0.898, 1.222). 100% is that body's own 64-generation full-space search.

Partly transferable, not a universal speed-up

On other bodies the child's 8 directions are far better than a random 8-dimensional subspace, so they do contain corrections shared across bodies, and they give the search a faster start. But they cover only part of what another body needs, so later in the search they hold it back and the full space catches up or overtakes. The sensible use is to search the child's directions for the first generations and then switch to the full space, or to compute U from several bodies; neither has been tested yet.

System

Two loops, and only c changes

Why not one general adapter

Corrections do not carry across motions

The obvious approach is a network that takes a motion's z0 and outputs the child's z. We tried supervised learning, an ES-trained adapter, and a closed-loop adapter that re-chooses z every second. On unseen motions the cost only drops to 0.85–0.99 of z0's. Searching the motion itself for 5 generations brings median L_align to 0.76 of z0's, and a full search to 0.40.

The best corrections for different motions are nearly orthogonal (cosine similarity about 0.03–0.06), and a single motion has many equally good solutions, so there is no consistent rule to learn. We spend the effort on making each motion's search fast instead of predicting its answer.

Limitations

What is not verified yet

  1. Small sample. The fast-search numbers come from 60 motions and one random seed; the cross-body test uses 24 motions per body.
  2. The principal directions come from the child only. The cross-body test covers 4 bodies, and U is computed from the child's searches alone. Computing U from several bodies, or using the child's directions for the first generations and then switching to the full space, has not been tried.
  3. The reference is not the true optimum. 100% is a 64-generation full-space search on the same motion. The cosine + heading cost has not fully converged by then (median best at generation 59), so the percentages are optimistic and some motions exceed 100%.
  4. Some solutions are sensitive to numerics. Re-running the same z with a different GPU batch size changes floating-point rounding slightly, the simulation amplifies it, and a few motions score very differently (one headstand goes from 0.34 to 1.07). The ladder chart and the videos on this page are re-runs at a different batch size; the convergence curve uses the scores recorded during search and may be optimistic.
  5. Timings include parallel load. Time per generation was measured with many searches running at once. Each search also spends about a minute loading the model and building environments; one process handling motions back to back avoids that.
